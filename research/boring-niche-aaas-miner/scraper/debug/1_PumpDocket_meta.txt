I run a concrete crew. Last week, I got a text from a truckie that stopped me where I stood.

Not a delivery time. Not a question about the pour. Not a docket.

A photo. And five words I can't stop thinking about.

Let me start from the beginning because this story is going to take a turn you won't expect.

I've been pouring commercial concrete for 28 years. Formwork, pours, the lot. The last six I've been running my own crew. Four blokes, sometimes six, a couple of utes and a smoko shed on whatever site we're on. We've dealt with pumps dying at 6 AM, a slab that went off in the heat before we could float it, an apprentice who reversed a bobcat into the boss's ute on his second day.

Normal stuff.

But about 6 months ago, something started happening that I'd never seen in 28 years on the tools.

Blokes started asking about the shed.

Not the fridge. Not the urn. Not the pie warmer.

The canister sitting next to the urn.

See, I keep a canister of orange powder on the bench in the smoko shed. There's a note taped to the front in my handwriting that says: "One scoop in water. For the 2 PM wall. It's not pre-workout."

Most blokes try it the way they'd take a biscuit from the tin. Because it's there.

But the talk at smoko started shifting.

"Slept through to the alarm. First time in months."

"Didn't hit the wall yesterday. Didn't even notice till I was driving home."

"Wife asked what was different. Told her nothing."

A steel-fixer we subbie in — 58, been on sites since he was 15 — started turning up with his own canister in his esky. Didn't say anything about it. Just did.

A young bloke on my crew, Danny, 24, in his second year, told me half his old man's crew drinks the same stuff. "Canister in every second lunchbox," he said, and went back to his sandwich.

Something was happening. Something real.

Then last month, something happened that I still think about at 3 AM — which, as you'll see, is a time of day I know very well.

A truckie named Col has been delivering to our pours for nine years. Late 50s. Solid. The kind of driver who backs a twelve-cubic agitator down a ramp first go, no spotter. Lately, quieter. Sat in the cab between loads with his eyes shut. Smiled when you waved him in, but there was something behind it. Something dimmed.

He did four loads for us Thursday. Three on Friday.

Friday arvo when I went to lock the shed, something was wrong.

The canister was gone.

Not the urn. Not the biscuits. The canister of powder. Gone.

I pulled up the camera footage.

4:48 PM. Col walked from the truck to the shed. Straight to the bench. Picked up the canister. Looked over his shoulder. Wrapped it in a rag. Tucked it inside his hi-vis. Walked back to the cab.

He took the whole thing.

I texted him: "Mate — cheers for the loads this week. Canister's gone from the shed. All good?"

Forty-five minutes later, my phone rang.

He wasn't crying. Blokes like Col don't. But his voice had gone somewhere I hadn't heard it go in nine years.

"I'm sorry, mate. I know that's not on. I'll pay for it. I just —"

"I haven't slept properly in three years. Not tired — I've been tired since 1994. This is the other thing. I'm on the ceiling at three in the morning, heart going like I've dropped something off the back of the truck, jaw clamped shut. Over nothing. Lyn asleep next to me."

"Then two o'clock every arvo I'm sitting in the queue at the plant and I can't keep my eyes open. Last month a bloke had to lean on his horn at a red light. I'd gone. Sitting at a light, engine running, gone."

"Doctor's done the bloods. Thyroid fine. Sugar fine. 'You're 58, Col. You drive twelve hours a day. Get more sleep.' Get more sleep. Sleep doesn't touch it. I get eight hours on a Sunday and Monday I feel like the truck's reversed over me."

"I looked it up. Three in the morning, phone under the doona. The internet gave me one word back — cortisol. So I bought the capsules off Amazon. Worked for three weeks. Then it turned the volume down on all of me. The stress and the drive. Both knobs, one hand. Flat as a slab and every ounce still in place. Threw them out."

"Then Thursday I had your orange stuff at smoko because the young fella said to. Twenty minutes later I'm sitting in the cab waiting on the pump and I notice my gut's let go. Not gone. Just — not braced against the belt. I didn't know it was braced."

"Thursday night I fell asleep without the ceiling routine. Friday I woke up to the alarm. The alarm. Lyn didn't know what to say to me."

"And Friday arvo I looked at that canister and I thought: I can't do Monday. I can't go back to sitting in the queue with my eyes shut."

"I panicked. I took it anyway. Because for two days I'd been a bloke who could get through the day, and I couldn't let that end."

I didn't say anything for a while.

Because he could have been telling my story. Word for word.

Fourteen months ago, I was Col.

Only mine finished in a hospital bed. Four days. Sue in a plastic chair beside me. A specialist who was extraordinary — I mean that, I take what he gave me every morning and I'm grateful for every tablet — who told me the thing I'd been ignoring for a year had finally stopped ignoring me.

The details were different but the shape was the same. Col nods off at a red light. I sat in my truck in my own driveway at 5 PM, engine off, and couldn't find enough to walk to my own front door. Col's wife doesn't know what to say to him. Sue had stopped asking me to do anything after dinner. Not even angry anymore. Just stopped. She'd say goodnight to the back of my head on the couch. Col's doctor said get more sleep. Mine said manage the stress — and he was right, he just couldn't tell me how, because nobody teaches a body how to switch back off. Same script. Same bloke sitting in a cab knowing in his bones that something else was going on.

What I need to tell you is what I found when I went looking for answers.

Because I didn't go looking until I visited Ray.

Ray trained me. 35 years on the tools. Retired at 54, "medical," and nobody ever says why. I went round on a Sunday. Small unit. TV on at 2 in the afternoon, curtains half drawn. Hasn't driven since March. "The stairs got longer," he said, and then apologised for his own stairs. Halfway through a sentence his cup tipped in his hand and he didn't notice. Same drop. Same body. Just a few more years on the clock.

I'd just sat with a man who ran out of time to figure it out.

That night — 3 AM, on the ceiling, phone under the covers so I wouldn't wake Sue — I typed the exact words: "why am I tired all day but wide awake at 3am." Not "cortisol supplement." I'd already wasted fourteen months on that — energy drinks, two before lunch then three, pre-workout off a younger bloke that had my hands shaking on the trowel, B12 shots, a sleep app my daughter put on my phone, and the cheap capsules that flattened me. None of it touched the 3 AM or the 2 PM or the truck. I researched WHY. Why was this happening to me?

And I found something that changed everything.

It was a long post on a forum for shift workers. Written by a doctor who'd spent twenty years doing occupational health for mine sites and FIFO crews before she stepped back. Not angry — just tired. Like a woman who'd spent two decades sending blokes back to camp with "get more rest" and watching them come back worse. She was clear about one thing: the doctors weren't wrong. The bloods were fine. The problem was that nobody had a plain-English name for what was actually happening, so nobody could tell a man how to fix it.

I almost scrolled past it. But her credentials checked out. Her writing was plain. No wellness words. Site words.

"Cortisol," she wrote, "is the smoke alarm. Not the fire."

"In a healthy body it has a curve. It rises at five in the morning so you can get up and get going. It falls at night so the body can stand down, repair, and refill the tank. That curve is what lets your legs recover, your head clear, your energy come back overnight."

I thought about Sunday. Eight hours. Monday like the concrete truck hit me.

"But for men who've spent decades under deadlines, heights, near-misses and heat, the nervous system learns that danger is normal. It never stands down. It idles high all night like a generator nobody's allowed to switch off. The body isn't broken. It's braced."

"And when it's braced long enough, cortisol loses its curve. Flat at 5 AM when you need it. Screaming at 3 AM when you don't. No fuel when you need it. Engine redlining when you're meant to be off. The 3 AM and the 2 PM aren't two problems. They're one."

I thought about the wall. Legs full of wet sand. Forty minutes standing still with a shovel, pretending to think.

"A body that believes it's in danger also stockpiles fuel through the middle — that's what it does under threat. It isn't a beer gut. It's rations. Which is why a man can eat less than he has in twenty years, do ten hours of physical work, and still watch the belt cut in by lunch. You can't work your way past a body that's protecting you."

I looked down at the belt. Same shirt for six years. Didn't sit right anymore.

"And here's what nobody tells the men who Google it at 3 AM: most of what they buy goes after the alarm. It forces the number down. That's pulling the batteries out while the house smoulders. It looks like action. It fixes nothing. And it dims the drive along with the stress — because both are running off the same system."

I thought about the capsules. Both knobs, one hand. Flat, foggy, and every ounce still in place.

She continued.

"The job isn't to mute the alarm. It's to give the curve back."

"Lifestyle changes help at the margins. But for a body that's been braced for years, telling a man to relax is like telling him to bail out a flooding boat with a coffee cup. You have to address the rhythm directly."

"Over the past two decades, a body of peer-reviewed research has examined specific compounds — adaptogens and phospholipids — that work at the HPA axis level to restore cortisol rhythm. Not calming herbs. Not general stress support. Compounds that have been shown in randomised controlled trials to bring the curve back."

"KSM-66® Ashwagandha — the most clinically studied form, with over 22 randomised controlled trials. Standardised to 5% withanolides. At the studied dose it restores the rhythm rather than flattening the man. That's the difference between this and the cheap capsules."

"Phosphatidylserine — one of the best-researched cortisol modulators there is. Shown to blunt the night-time spike — the 3 AM one. Most bottles skip it entirely."

"Rhodiola Rosea — for a hair-trigger system. Rebuilds the body's ability to stand itself down."

"L-Theanine, Myo-Inositol, Turmeric — each addressing a downstream consequence of running hot for years: an overactive nervous system, blood sugar that's never level, inflammation that never quite settles."

"Together they don't sedate the body. They restore the rhythm. And when the rhythm comes back, the alarm stops going off on its own — because the house isn't smouldering anymore."

I put the phone down at 4. Picked it up again at 5, which is when I'm supposed to have cortisol and never do.

I found the product she'd referenced. A complete formula — every compound at the studied dose, in one powder. One scoop into a glass of water in the morning. Orange. That's the whole protocol.

I ordered Harmonia.

Felt stupid doing it. 28 years on the tools, ordering orange powder off my phone at 5 AM because of a forum post. Sue thought it was an electrolyte drink. I didn't correct her.

Twenty minutes.

I mixed the first one at the ute Monday before a big pour. Twenty minutes in, I noticed my gut had let go. Not gone. Just not braced against the belt anymore. I hadn't known it was braced. Twenty-eight years of reading a slab by looking at it, and I could not read my own body.

Danny walked past, looked at the canister, and said, "Oh, that's the stuff my old man drinks." Then he grinned. "Told you."

He had told me. Three months earlier, at the tailgate. I'd laughed at him. Gave him grief for a week about it. Now I was standing there drinking orange powder because an apprentice told me to.

I went and checked the formwork so nobody would see my face.

Week one, I fell asleep without the ceiling routine. Just — went under.

Week two, I woke up to the alarm. The alarm. Jaw unclenched. Belt not cutting in by lunch.

Week three, I noticed at quarter past three that I hadn't hit the wall. Hadn't stood still with the shovel. Hadn't gone looking for a third can.

Week four, I pulled into the driveway at ten past five, turned the engine off, and got out of the truck. Just got out. Walked inside. Sue looked up from the bench like she'd heard a noise she didn't recognise.

By week six, I had my follow-up with the specialist.

Sue came with me. He scrolled through the results. Stopped. Scrolled back.

"You look different," he said. "Before I even opened this. You walked in here differently."

I shrugged. "I feel different."

"Your cortisol rhythm's back where it should be. Inflammatory markers are down. Everything's moving in the right direction."

He was quiet for a bit. Then: "Whatever you changed, keep doing it."

He didn't laugh at the powder. He said most of the men sitting in his waiting room were stuck exactly where I'd been.

That was eight months ago.

Month two, the day stopped following me home. Near-misses stopped replaying when I shut my eyes. The scale stopped climbing — then the belt went back a hole. The old hole. The one with the worn groove in it. Arms filled back out. Work shirts sit right.

And week nine — this is the one I can't say without my throat going tight — Sue put her fork down at dinner and said, "You're here. You haven't been here at dinner in a year."

I'm not the bloke who sat in the truck. I'm the bloke she married.

A few weeks after the follow-up, I was mixing my scoop at the ute at smoko and I looked over at the crew. Blokes at the tailgate with their eyes shut. The phone going at 5:30 with a wife asking if he's left yet. I know that look. I know that phone call.

I went to the shops that arvo and bought a canister. Filled it with Harmonia. Taped a note on the front: "One scoop in water. For the 2 PM wall. It's not pre-workout." Put it on the bench next to the urn.

Didn't overthink it. Just did it.

That was six months ago. And the smoko talk I told you about at the beginning? Now you know why it doesn't sound like pre-workout talk.

Because it isn't pre-workout.

It's what got me out of the truck.

So here's what I told Col on the phone that Friday:

"Keep the canister. Every bit of it. But I'm sending you the link so you can order your own. Because that canister's going to run out, and when it does I don't want you back in the queue at the plant with your eyes shut."

He laughed, sort of. "Deal."

I sent him the link that arvo. He ordered two. Said he'd have got three but it was sold out — which is why I keep two in the house.

Last week — exactly three weeks after that Friday — he texted me a photo.

His truck. In his driveway. Engine off. Nobody in it. Taken from inside the house, through the flyscreen — so you know where he was standing when he took it.

Five words underneath the photo:

"Inside by five past five."

That's the message from the beginning of this story. That's what stopped me where I stood. Not the theft. Not the confession. A photo of an empty truck, sent by a bloke who used to sit in it.

I printed it. It's on the fridge in the shed, next to the canister.

If you're reading this, I know exactly where you are.

On the ceiling at eleven minutes past three, heart going over nothing. Dead at two. Stuck in the truck at five. Eating less than you have in twenty years, doing more physical work than any gym bloke you know, and the belt cutting in by lunch anyway. Saying goodnight to the back of your own head.

And someone keeps saying it's age. It's the job. It's the price of what happened to you. Get more sleep. The bloods look fine.

But the bloods don't sit in the truck. You do.

And you don't have to wear a hard hat for this to be your problem. Truckies. Nurses. Shift workers. Dads doing sixty hours. Anyone who's been running hot long enough. Or the bloke sitting in the truck in your driveway right now — if that's who you're reading this for.

One path: keep telling yourself it's just the job now, and turn into Ray one stair at a time.

Another path: give your body the thing it's been waiting fourteen months to hear. The emergency is over. You can stand down. Bring the curve back with a complete formula that addresses the rhythm — not the alarm. Watch the 3 AM go quiet. Watch the 2 PM wall not turn up. Watch yourself get out of the truck.

I chose the second path. Col chose the second path. Danny's old man's crew chose it before either of us did.

Ray didn't get the choice.

— Wayne
